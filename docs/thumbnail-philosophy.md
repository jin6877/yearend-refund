A visual philosophy of the second copy — the impression that arrives through carbon paper, one sheet removed from the hand that pressed it. Everything is ruled, counted and boxed, and every line carries the faint misregistration of a duplicate: the same stroke, struck twice, a hair apart. The work treats bureaucratic paper as an instrument of patience rather than tedium, and it should look meticulously crafted, as if each rule were drawn with a straightedge over many quiet evenings.

Space is a grid of cells, never a void. The canvas is divided into narrow vertical registers of equal width, and the eye reads them left to right like a year of entries. Inside the registers, short horizontal marks accumulate in columns of different heights, a sediment of small deductions. The density is the point: hundreds of marks placed with painstaking attention, none decorative, each one obeying the same spacing so that the pattern reads as record, not ornament. One register breaks the rhythm. It is wider, emptier, set apart by a doubled rule, and it is where the composition comes to rest.

Color is restricted to the materials on a clerk's desk. White paper. A single violet-indigo, the exact ink of carbon copies, used for every rule, numeral and letter, in two strengths: the pressed line and its fainter ghost. And one vermilion, the color of a seal pad, which appears exactly once. Nothing else is allowed onto the sheet. The vermilion should feel like an event; its scarcity is what gives it weight, and its placement must be the product of deep expertise in balance, not chance.

Scale is quiet and then sudden. Numerals and labels are tiny, set in a thin serif or a clinical sans, the size of print on a form. The title is set wide, letters held apart the way official forms space their headings, so the word becomes a ruled band rather than a shout. Against this restraint, the seal is large, round, slightly rotated, its double ring imperfect the way a real impression is imperfect. That single gesture is the only thing on the canvas that was not drawn with a ruler.

Composition is balanced asymmetrically: weight of accumulated marks on the left, release on the right. Margins are generous and exact, nothing touches the edge, nothing overlaps unintentionally, and every alignment has been refined until it is invisible. The finished sheet should read as an artifact from a careful discipline, a ledger of something usually felt rather than measured, rendered with master-level execution and the calm authority of a document that has been checked twice.
